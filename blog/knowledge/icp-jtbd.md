# ICP & JTBD
Distilled from: the company's customer-interview study of current clients (segments and jobs), its
public segment definition and the target-audience layer of its positioning document.

## Applicability
- Segments, jobs, pains and voice-of-customer language: no restriction.
- **The product descriptions and job phrasing in this card use the buyer's own words — «get
  contractor invoices approved», «onboarding», «compliance», «freelancers».** That is the buyer's vocabulary and
  the JTBD substrate. Before it reaches any page in the company's voice it must be translated per

- **Approval wording:** never describe Example Co as the party that approves invoices; the

## ICP layer — READ WITH the JTBD segments below
The two sources describe **different segments, not a contradiction**:
- Public segment definition, core: service and product companies of 20–500 people whose work is
  partly done by independent contractors in several countries; the decision is made collegially
  (founder, finance, operations). Entry situation: the number of contractors grows, and the manual
  routine of agreements in mailboxes and invoices in a spreadsheet stops coping.
- Positioning document, target: **from 10+ active contractors.**
- **Priority rule: the more active contractors a company has, the more interesting it is for
  Example Co.** Both segments are valid; the larger one is the more valuable.
- Product-to-scale mapping: **up to ~10 contractors** — a spreadsheet and a shared folder cope (the
  risk is small; we do not sell here, we only explain) · **from ~10** — approvals start to get lost
  and the chasing begins · **from ~50** — a head of finance or a controller appears, the founder no
  longer decides alone, and exports, roles and approval routes come to the front. Example Co
  sells ONE product — **Example Workspace**; anything else is an internal roadmap item and is not
  described as part of the offer. See `positioning.md` §4.
- **Selling thresholds (deliberately soft — the figures are a guide, not a rule):** the main
  audience is companies with **10+ active contractors** in more than one country; **we start
  selling from about 10 contractors**; companies **below 10 — we educate, but do not necessarily
  sell**. In cold traffic the cut-off is a company of about 20 people.
- **ICP A / ICP B — the current working split, use alongside the four JTBD segments below:**
  **ICP A** — service companies (agencies, consultancies, production teams) that engage contractors
  per client project, so the line-up changes every few weeks.
  **ICP B** — product companies with a stable group of long-term contractors abroad.
  Example Workspace is the flagship for BOTH. The JTBD segments below describe buying behaviour and
  jobs; ICP A/B describes the engagement pattern. They are complementary, not competing.
- **Entry situation:** the team grows and spreads across countries, and manual handling of
  agreements and invoices stops coping. **They search for «a contractor agreement template» or «an
  invoice tracker» — and our task is to explain that the problem is the trail, not the single
  document.** This is the single most useful sentence in the positioning document for content
  strategy: it names the search intent AND the gap the article has to close.
- Prospective segments: small international teams with manual and mixed paperwork routines;
  fast-growing startups with a growing volume of agreements; large distributed teams (50+
  contractors) that ask for exports and automation; companies that have outgrown a spreadsheet
  and a shared folder.
- Role-based entry points and the scale axis (up to ~30 → 30–50 → 50+) live in `positioning.md`
  ("Role-based entry points") — do not duplicate here.

## Rules (verbatim-critical)
- In the buyer's own words, Example Co is "the tool where we onboard freelancers abroad, keep their contracts and get their invoices approved so finance can close the month without chasing anyone". **[INTERNAL phrasing — for public text use the category formula and the translation table in `positioning.md`.]**
- The customer is the BUSINESS (B2B): the buyers and users are the founder, the head of finance, the controller, the operations manager, people operations and the accountant who closes the month. Articles must speak to these roles, not to the contractors who sign and invoice (contractors are a separate audience — see the last section).
- There are 4 valid ICP segments + 1 explicitly NON-ICP segment. Do NOT pitch to the non-ICP "workaround seekers" (companies that want paperwork to disguise something). They are labelled: "Not an ICP: unreliable, not inclined to a productive long-term relationship or expansion, opaque." Never frame Example Co as a way to make an employment relationship look like contracting or to produce documents after the fact.
- The PRIMARY ICP (★ "Growing teams") is the one growth communications must be built around. Research conclusion: a growth strategy has to settle on one main ICP and build the core communications around it.
- Core jobs differ by segment but reduce to: (1) every contractor works under a current, signed agreement that can be found; (2) invoices arrive in one format and are approved by the right person before finance pays; (3) the full document set can be handed to an auditor, an investor or an acquirer without a rescue project; (4) for project businesses — many short engagements are opened and closed without the coordinator drowning in admin.
- "Vendor trust" was rated very high by ALL ICPs. It spans: the official site, the pricing page, the quality of the public help centre and sample templates, the top of search results, publications in trade media, opinion in professional communities, the founders' public presence. Content, site and PR are a real buying factor, not vanity: a buyer who cannot find a clear public answer assumes the product cannot give one either.
- Order in documents is load-bearing, not decorative: signed agreements, an approval trail, exports that reconcile with the ledger, passing an auditor's or an investor's document request. For the "established" segments tolerance for gaps is zero — a single invoice without an agreement behind it is a deal-breaker for the whole tool.
- Proof assets to lean on (positioning wording): pricing published in full on the pricing page, public sample templates, a public help centre and changelog, named authors with operational experience who speak at finance-operations meetups. Never customer counts or growth figures that are not published on the site.
- The contractor's experience is a critical, often decisive deal-breaker for ICPs — especially for mature companies, which will route around the tool to keep a valued specialist. Do not downplay how easy it is for a contractor to sign and to submit an invoice.
- "A named person who answers" is a stated selection criterion: buyers want a human contact during setup and a quick answer when an approval is stuck. Example Co positions as a hands-on partner.

## Notes / context

### Research basis
- Internal interviews with CURRENT clients: a small set of companies, one or two respondents each (mostly heads of finance, plus operations managers, a controller, founders and one people-operations lead). Company and respondent names are not recorded in this card; quotes are paraphrased.

### Expected results (jobs) and their importance, in buyers' own words (verbatim — SEO/GEO value)
- "Every contractor abroad has a signed contract I can find in under a minute" — importance: very high, named unprompted in most interviews.
- "Invoices come in looking the same, with the same fields, so nobody retypes them" — very high.
- "The person who owns the budget approves the invoice before it reaches finance, and I can prove it later" — very high.
- "When the auditor asks for the contract behind a payment, I send a link, not a week of my life" — very high.
- "I close the month without writing to fifteen people to ask whether their freelancers have invoiced."
- "I know which agreements run out next month before the contractor reminds me."
- "My team stops spending days on contractor paperwork and reconciliation" — medium.
- "The export goes into our accounting software without a clean-up spreadsheet in between."
- Off-the-record real business goals: keep a lean core team and scale with contractors without building a back office; look orderly in front of investors; keep working with trusted specialists wherever they live.

### Selection criteria (scored across primary segments)
- Setup help and support — a named contact, quick answers, help with moving existing agreements in.
- Ease for approvers — an approval in a couple of steps from a notification (NICE-TO-HAVE for the people who run the tool; decisive for budget owners, who otherwise ignore it).
- Ease for contractors — signing and submitting an invoice without training or an account manager.
- Template flexibility — own clauses, own statement-of-work structure, the company's counsel can edit.
- Export fit — the export matches the chart of accounts and the accounting software in use.
- Price predictability — a published price, a clear rule for what counts as an active contractor (dominant for the larger and project-based segments).
- Stability — nothing gets lost, no duplicate invoices (treated as base hygiene by larger segments).
- Vendor trust (see Rules).
- Coverage — contractors in the countries the company actually works with; agreement and invoice fields that fit them.

### Segment 1 — ★ "Growing teams" (PRIMARY ICP)
- A company of roughly 20–100 people that has grown past the point where the founder knows every contractor. Jobs: (1) every contractor has a current signed agreement; (2) invoices are approved by the budget owner before finance pays.
- User in the tool: the first finance or operations hire, sometimes still the founder. Domain understanding is usually shallow — they mix up an agent-of-record arrangement, an employer of record and a staffing agency, are weak on worker-status tests, and are not ready to advise their contractors on anything. The contractor's experience matters and can be a deal-breaker.
- Previously solved the jobs with a shared folder, a spreadsheet and a chat thread, or did not see a problem at all. Awareness appeared WITH growth or with the first outside check: an investor's document request, a new accountant, a client's procurement questionnaire. Looking orderly at the next check is critical.
- Newcomers to contractor-management software; ask for guidance on what a complete contractor record should contain.
- Roughly 10–50 active contractors; high potential to grow within months. Senior people approve invoices themselves. They keep the contractor list in a spreadsheet and do not see that as a problem (flexibility matters more to them than automation).
- Typical examples: a software consultancy that doubled its bench in a year; a research boutique after its first large international study.
- How to sell: be the practical guide for the next stage of growth; be perceived as people who have done this paperwork (clear demos, quick setup help, expert communication). Frame the benefit as before/after against the old routine (agreements in mailboxes, approvals in chat). Stress the trail: a signed agreement behind every invoice, a named approver, a set that can be handed to a checker. Present proof assets (published price, public templates, help centre).

### Segment 2 — "Established finance teams" / Mature companies with settled processes
- A mature company of roughly 100–500 people that is formalising its corporate structure. Same jobs: (1) agreements for every contractor; (2) saving the team's time on documents and reconciliation.
- A harder decision process than in growing teams. The head of finance has a team; decisions are made at C-level, sometimes without the founders. LEGAL COUNSEL always takes part. Strong domain knowledge of contractor engagement and its legal side; guidance is only a nice-to-have, but editable templates and a clean version history are valued.
- 50+ active contractors, several users of different levels: head of finance, controller, accountants, operations managers. Deep domain knowledge among decision-makers; hands-on users may know less. PRICE matters far more than for growing teams (larger volumes, higher financial literacy). They may run more than one tool side by side and may have unique requirements per process. They do not search for solutions — vendors approach them constantly. They keep a fallback routine ready.
- A sales cycle several times longer, but a much higher lifetime value. Contractor complaints are a likely deal-breaker (they will route around the tool to keep a specialist).
- Typical examples: a product company with long-term contractors in a dozen countries; a group of agencies with a shared finance function.
- How to sell: project domain expertise (it builds trust). Emphasise editable templates, version history and the approval trail — "with this set of documents you are likely to get the tool past your counsel and to answer an auditor's request without a project." Present proof assets. Stress a simple contractor-side flow, quick support, the help centre and a named contact.

### Segment 3 — "Founder-run companies" (the secondary reader)
- A company of up to about 30 people where the founder handles contractor paperwork personally. Same two jobs as Segment 1, with one difference: the buyer, the user and the approver are the same person.
- A handful to a couple of dozen contractors, often long-standing. No finance hire yet; an outside accountant closes the month and asks for documents once a quarter. The decision is made in a day, usually after a specific painful evening.
- Order in documents matters less as a principle and more as a way to stop being the bottleneck: "get it out of my inbox". Expect no patience for configuration — the first agreement has to go out during the first session. Price-sensitive in absolute terms, but indifferent to small differences between vendors.
- Typical example: a design studio whose founder signs every statement of work and forwards every invoice to the accountant by hand.
- How to sell: speak about evenings and inboxes, not about controls. Show the first agreement and the first approved invoice, nothing else. Do not lead with audit vocabulary — it reads as a problem for a later, larger company. Content that works: checklists and worked examples the founder can use even without the product.

### Segment 4 — "Project businesses" / Agencies and production teams with high contractor turnover
- A company of any size within the range whose contractor line-up changes with every client project. Jobs: (1) open and close many short engagements without the coordinator drowning in admin; (2) save the team's time on documents and reconciliation.
- Key driver: the volume of small, repetitive paperwork — many short statements of work, many small invoices. They will accept a less polished tool if it removes retyping. Order in documents as a value is a low-rated nice-to-have until a client's procurement asks for it. Decision-makers do not use the tool themselves; they delegate to project coordinators.
- Long in the market, a large pool of occasional contractors (translators, interviewers, editors, technicians). Industries: translation, market research, media production, events. Deep knowledge of their own routine; already have a home-made system of forms and spreadsheets. Counsel, operations and finance all take part; a decision can take months. PRICE is key, and specifically the rule for what counts as an active contractor in a month. Templates are often their own and non-negotiable.
- Extra criteria: adapting the invoice fields to the company's context (for example, a project code on every invoice); quality support at coordinator level, where the daily questions arise.
- Typical example: a translation company with hundreds of linguists, most of whom invoice in only a few months of the year.
- How to sell: the same long-cycle, many-touches, network-based approach as for established finance teams; lead with the price rule (charged only for months with an approved invoice) and with flexibility of templates and fields.

### NON-ICP — "Workaround seekers" (do not target)
- Actual job: make an arrangement look different on paper from what it is — full-time staff presented as contractors, agreements dated earlier than they were signed, invoices produced after a payment was made. Secretive, not domain experts, tolerant of risk, interested only while the tool serves the workaround. May ask to edit signed documents, remove the approval history or back-date an export. Often several legal entities; the decision-maker is the founder, the work may be delegated to an assistant.
- Explicitly NOT an ICP. If engaged at all: explain plainly how the trail works and that it cannot be rewritten, do not pressure, do not try to expose them.

### Additional segmentation axes (use for context/targeting, not as primary ICP)
- By scenario in the tool: 1. Companies (1.1 founders and executives, 1.2 finance, 1.3 operations and people operations, 1.4 budget owners who approve); 2. Contractors (individuals, sole traders, small companies invoicing for one person).
- By headcount of active contractors: up to 10 / 10–50 / 50+ / 150+.
- By contractor type: long-term individual contractors who work like part of the team; one-off contractors for a single deliverable; a mix of both; a large pool of occasional contractors (translators, interviewers, moderators); contractors that are themselves small companies.
- By contractor geography: one or two countries abroad; many countries; a mix of domestic and foreign contractors.
- By industry: 1. software and technology services; 2. translation and research; 3. media and content production; 4. agencies and consultancies; 5. product companies with long-term contractors. NOTE: the industry does NOT directly drive how the tool is used; the scenario does. For sales, use industry context; for product, the scenario is the priority and the industry is irrelevant.

### Key insights / objections (cross-segment)
- The value of the tool is HEAVILY context-dependent; sell to the client's desired result, not to features. Demo insight: focus on uncovering the client's key goal instead of walking through functions. Proposed demo frame — ask which of 3 goals is the priority: (a) every contractor under a signed agreement; (b) invoices approved by the right person before payment; (c) less team time on documents and reconciliation. Hypothesis: build 3–5 anchor messages per ICP job.
- Adapt the approach to the lead's role: operations manager → ease for contractors and approvers, a simple interface; head of finance / founder → the trail, stability, a predictable price.
- Switching inertia: almost everyone names the difficulty of moving existing agreements and habits; inertia keeps them where they are even when they are less than satisfied. The more the routine depends on the tool, the better it retains. Watch the switching window — a new head of finance, a failed document request from an investor, a client's procurement questionnaire are such triggers; stay in contact with target decision-makers.
- Counsel takes part (openly or not) in almost every selection. Include counsel-relevant content and reviews early: "Show this to your lawyers so they have no questions." Hypothesis: a dedicated pack for counsel — a page, a deck, a deep FAQ on templates, signatures and version history.
- Buyers run their own background check: they read reviews, look at the vendor's public activity and at the people behind it. Choosing a tool is also about the employee's own standing — they carry the risk of the choice; trust in the vendor's reputation is an irrational but real factor.
- A recommendation from a peer is the top information source for most respondents; a referral programme could be the first acquisition channel. Hypothesis: a structured referral project.
- Positioning has to flex across ICPs and sub-segments (role, industry, engagement pattern); in every communication know which segment and which problem is being addressed.
- The moment a company becomes aware of the problem is strategically important: reach future buyers BEFORE they start an active search, so that Example Co is first on the list.
- Objection heard in every segment: "we already have a spreadsheet and it works." Answer with the question a checker asks — "show me the agreement behind this invoice and who approved it" — not with a feature list.
- Objection from counsel: "we will not use someone else's contract." Answer: the templates are the company's own once edited; the tool stores and versions them. **[Never phrase this as legal review or lawyer-approved wording — see `positioning.md` §1.]**

### Product pains (affect business perception, useful as content angles)
- Low awareness of existing features (interviewees did not know about reminders or the bulk import of existing agreements) → invest in client education, help-centre content, feature announcements.
- Strong demand for clarity on what "an active contractor" means in a given month — buyers want to estimate the bill before the month ends, ideally inside the tool.
- A convenient help centre and a guided first session matter for both business users and contractors.
- The contractor's first invoice is the most fragile moment and shapes how the business sees the tool: contractors get confused by required fields, postpone, and send a file by email instead; sometimes one contractor delays a month-end close. Hypothesis: a multilingual help section for contractors, a field-by-field guide to the invoice form, an optional walkthrough video; show per contractor on the business dashboard whether the agreement is signed and the first invoice submitted.
