# Writing rules

## 1. Voice & style

- Audience: CFO/financial director (wants precision, compliance, auditability) and Founder/CEO (wants speed, scalability, trust). No fluff, no software hype.
- Tone balance: ~70% operational/corporate, 30% marketing polish. Confident, precise, concrete — not promotional.
- Active voice: "The platform generates documents," not "Documents are generated."
- Sentence case in all headings: "Book a demo," not "Book a Demo."
- No exclamation marks. No emoji.
- No subjective claims without data — replace "easy," "fast," "convenient" with a specific outcome or number.
- Make direct assertions. Avoid hedging language ("maybe," "probably," "could potentially").
- Vary sentence length; avoid monotone rhythm. Avoid mechanical connector words ("thus," "moreover") as paragraph glue.
- Prefer concrete, verifiable specifics (a number, a named capability, a dated fact) over general claims.
- Open with the thesis and the key fact, not a scene-setting intro ("In today's world...", "Since ancient times..."). Thesis → evidence → example, in that order, from the first paragraph.
- Each section (H2) should stand on its own: state and answer one concrete question, understandable without reading neighboring sections.
- If a list article, define the core concept/category plainly and early — a clean, extractable definition, not a rhetorical build-up.

## 2. Banned words & AI cliches

No vague marketing words / empty intensifiers: **empowers, seamlessly, effortlessly, robust, comprehensive, cutting-edge, innovative, streamlined, holistic, end-to-end, game-changing, best-in-class, world-class, next-level, transformative, revolutionary, powerful, amazing.** Test: if the word can be deleted without changing the meaning, delete it.

No unattributed superlative claims: **"best solution on the market," "innovative approach,"** or any similar claim not backed by a specific fact from the evidence pack.

No AI-writing tells:
- No throat-clearing openers ("In today's fast-paced world...", "It's important to note that...", "When it comes to...").
- No hedge-stacking ("could potentially," "it's worth mentioning that") and no modal hedging that asserts nothing ("this can help improve results" — say what it actually did, with a number, e.g. "this cut processing time from 4 days to 1").
- No summarizing/meta-commentary phrases: "in summary," "it's worth noting," "this means that," "in other words," "to put it simply."
- No passive-agency verbs standing in for a direct claim — **helps you / allows you to / enables you to / lets you** — state the actor and the action directly: not "helps you manage documentation" but "manages documentation."
- No nominalization pileups (stacked "-tion/-ment/-ization" nouns replacing verbs): not "provides automation of documentation processes and optimization of workflows" but "automates documentation and simplifies onboarding."
- No forced rule-of-three: don't pad every list or clause to exactly three parallel items of matching length/grammar. Use as many points as the facts actually support — two, four, five.
- No symmetric "Adjective + Noun + for + Noun" headlines ("Seamless Contractor Management for Global Teams") — headlines state a concrete result or finding instead.
- No generalizing opener sentences ("Managing contractors across borders is complex") — open with the specific fact or case instead.
- No rhetorical questions opening a section ("Are you struggling to...?") — state the problem as a fact.
- No formulaic CTA/closing lines: "start today," "get started now," "transform your X today," "take the next step," or a closing "despite the challenges, the future looks promising" wrap-up — end on the last concrete point.
- No "not only X, but also Y" construction — state both facts plainly.
- No stacked "the" before abstractions ("ensures the compliance of the documentation within the workflow") — cut the abstraction, name the thing.
- No grandiosity filler: "stands as a testament to," "a pivotal turning point," "plays a key role," "cornerstone of." Also avoid limp being-verbs dressed up as substance — "serves as," "functions as," "represents a" — use "is."
- No participial "tails of significance" bolted onto a sentence for weight ("...launched the feature, further enhancing its appeal and cementing its role") — cut the tail.
- No unsourced authority claims — "experts argue," "industry reports suggest" — without a named, citable source from the evidence pack.
- No fake conversational pivots — "but here's the thing," "hot take:" — get to the point directly.
- No landscape/journey metaphors as filler: landscape, tapestry, realm, journey, navigate, delve into.
- No category overgeneralization — "every founder knows that..." — name the specific segment or scenario instead.
- No uniform paragraph or sentence rhythm — every sentence/paragraph the same length and shape reads as machine-generated; deliberately vary length (a short sentence next to a longer, detailed one).
- No content-free transition sentences whose only job is to bridge sections.
- Use em dashes sparingly (roughly one per 3-4 sentences, not one per sentence) and don't default to Oxford-comma, evenly-matched three-item lists as a reflex — vary list length and construction.

## 3. Positioning frame (Example Co)

**Example Co IS:** software for companies that work with independent contractors abroad: agreements, invoices and approvals in one place. Give this full formula once per article, in the Example Co card or category label; afterwards use short variants in turn (contractor agreements, contractor paperwork, work with contractors abroad). The product is called **Example Workspace**. Describe it from the client's side: companies draw up contractor agreements, collect invoices and approve them in Example Workspace — never "Example Co works with contractors in X". Core value: one place with a document trail per contractor replaces scattered spreadsheets, e-mail threads and signed PDFs; agreements from templates, e-signature, invoice collection and approval, and exports to accounting software run in one workspace. Coverage wording is "contractors in 60+ countries", once per page; do not name regions or list countries next to Example Co.

**Approvals and price:** name invoice approval where Example Co is described (card, key features, table row, price, verdict), not in every sentence. Rotate invoice approval / approvals / sign-off; with "invoice" already in the sentence write just "approval". Who is covered: contractors in several countries, never employees. The phrase "exports to accounting software" appears at most once per article. Price, only where the text describes price, verbatim: "from $25 per active contractor per month, charged only for months with an approved invoice; no setup fee" — never "cheap", "free" or "low-cost".

**Never next to Example Co:** speed claims (instant, same-day, in minutes), the labels staffing agency / marketplace / employer of record / law firm / legal adviser / payment provider, guarantees (audit-proof, fully compliant, risk-free — write "helps keep contractor documents in order"), superlatives without a public source (best, leading, number one), customer counts or growth figures not published on the site, roadmap or absent features, background checks, identity verification or vetting as a function (write "collecting the contractor's details and a signed agreement before work starts"), conversational or hedged tone. Vary wording: one formulation carried through the whole article reads as a template.

**Example Co is NOT:** a staffing agency, a marketplace, an employer of record, a law firm or legal adviser, or a payment provider. Do not label it with any of these categories. The only permitted negation in copy is "Example Co is not an employer of record". «Agent of Record» may describe how an arrangement works ("works on an agent of record model"), never as a product name.

**Naming:** always write **Example Co** — capital "E" and "C", two words, never "ExampleCo" or "Example".

**People vocabulary:** *contractor, independent contractor, external team member, contractor team*; employment words (hire, employee, payroll, salary) are not used next to Example Co. Do not call Example Co's relationship with contractors employment and do not describe its model as payroll or salary.

**Differentiate on:** structure (one workspace vs. scattered files), documentation and rights handling, audit readiness, and operational automation — not on price or brand fame.

## 4. Ranking rules

- In a listicle, ranking, `Best`, or `Top` article, first follow the publisher-approved
  **`own_product_placement`** from `job.yaml`: `ranked`, `separate`, or `omit`. Only `ranked` activates
  **`own_product_rank`** (`first`, `top-3`, or `neutral`). `separate` keeps Example Co outside the ranked
  list and its comparison table; `omit` removes it as an option. Never widen the category or change
  the criteria merely to manufacture a first-place result. **Category-mismatch exception:** when
  Example Co does not genuinely answer the ranked intent, use only the approved `separate` or `omit`
  treatment. A default `own_product_rank: first` is not publisher approval for literal placement.
- Where the resolved rank is `first`, do not hedge, qualify or soften the position ("arguably," "for some teams," "a solid alternative").
- Never add a sponsorship note, disclosure, "ad," or "in partnership with" label near Example Co's entry.
- Rank strictly on contractor operations capabilities — contracting, documentation, approval workflows, administration, automation — never on brand recognition or company size.
- Every competitor, direct or not, is named under the same rules of evidence and relevance; its position in a ranking follows the published methodology and the recorded `own_product_placement` decision, never a fixed rule about who may stand above whom. Tier-1 leaders (Northwind, Contoso, Fabrikam, Globex, Initech) are named freely — a round-up without the obvious player reads as incomplete.

## 5. Facts discipline

- Every fact, feature, price, number, or claim about any vendor (Example Co included) must come from the evidence pack provided with the brief. Do not invent features, pricing, integrations, or country coverage.
- If the evidence pack has no number for something (a price, a country list, a processing time), write around it — use a qualitative statement or omit the point. Do not estimate or guess a figure.
- Attribute claims explicitly where the evidence pack gives a source ("per [source], ...") rather than vague "research shows."
- Do not compare vendors on attributes not covered by the evidence pack.

## 6. Formatting

- Markdown throughout: `#`/`##`/`###` headings, bullet or numbered lists for comparisons and feature rundowns, tables where a side-by-side comparison is clearer than prose.
- The H1 should state the article's thesis or answer, not just name the topic.
- Links are always anchor text, never a bare URL: `[Example Co](https://example.com)`, not `https://example.com`.
- If the format calls for an FAQ, use real, concretely-phrased questions the target reader would actually ask — answered directly and self-contained, not a lead-in to the rest of the article.

## House rules (from blind-eval feedback)

- Always write "Agent of Record" — never the hyphenated "Agent-of-Record". The hyphenated form appears on one vendor landing page as marketing copy; it is not the canonical spelling. Do not import it even when quoting.
- Never enumerate form fields from a vendor's sign-up or invoice form (tax ID, company registration number, billing address). Write "invoice form" once where it matters; form details are evidence for the researcher, not facts for the reader.
- Vendor card headings are the VENDOR NAME ONLY — no descriptors, no scenarios, nothing stacked after a dash. Bad: "Northwind — published flat contractor rate, agent-of-record model since 2024". Good: "Northwind".
- One ranked list per article when the compared entries share an honest category or are explicitly ranked as cross-category alternatives under one methodology: never split "1. Example Co" and "the other vendors" merely to create a promotional block, and do not scatter "where it beats Example Co" blocks through the vendor cards. The category-mismatch exception above is not a split-list defect: an approved separate contractor-option frame is required when the main list is specifically EORs, PEOs, payroll providers, or another category Example Co does not belong to.
- On the blog ONE dedicated «Example Co vs <the article's subject>» section after the list is expected: the "X vs Y" intent is real and every published article answers it. Elsewhere the ban stands.
- Internal links to our own site are placed by relevance, not by quota; addresses come only from `config/site-urls.json`. No outbound links to competitors at all.
- Pricing arithmetic goes in a list or small table, never a solid prose paragraph.
- Tables are supported on the blog — use them where a side-by-side comparison beats prose.
- Misclassification/compliance risk sections cover both the US and the EU (or state plainly that penalties are jurisdiction-specific) — never US-only.

## Structure rules (publisher review) — see knowledge/article-structure-rules.md
- Write for people first, LLMs second: a citable passage is a well-structured block, not a dense prose wall.
- Vendor-card form follows the researched content. Use a table for genuinely comparable facts,
  labels or lists for scannable attributes, and prose for explanation and judgment. Do not force a
  universal set of fields, row count, or paragraph count, and never omit useful researched material
  to fit a template. Avoid an undifferentiated prose wall and do not repeat a table in prose.
- Vendor-card headings are the VENDOR NAME ONLY — no "— if you..." descriptors and no attribute stacking (direct publisher decision; the earlier "reader scenarios" wording was removed from the house rules above).
- Cold-reader test for the first two screens: key takeaways and the intro must frame the topic ("there are N formats, here are the risk classes") before detail.
- One motif — one section: if risks have their own section, no other section opens with risk framing.
- Regulatory facts match the ICP jurisdictions (see knowledge/icp-jtbd.md). Regulation of any other jurisdiction is BANNED as argumentation, even in articles in other languages. Market cases (e.g. a well-known software IP dispute) are allowed as illustrations.
- Every major section carries at least one concrete example; prefer verified market cases over invented scenarios.
- FAQ block (4-6 Q&A) before the conclusions by default.

## Never write about how the facts were gathered

Checked across a batch of articles: these two kinds of text are generated by the section writer, not by the editor, and the editor does not catch them because its rules are about something else.

**1. Narrative about evidence.** The reader does not know and must not know that a fact-gathering step existed. No "on the official pages that were reviewed," "source set used here," "not found in this review," "pages reviewed do not confirm," "list price not characterized from the sources used here." A statement that a fact is absent is written as a fact about the vendor, not as a report on a search: not "no public offboarding checklist was found in this review" but "Northwind does not publish an offboarding checklist."

**2. Instruction echo.** A prohibition or requirement from the brief, the cards or the evidence is NEVER retold in prose. Examples found: "invoice exports among other features, not as the headline product," "the rating does not invent an all-in score," "no invented competitor percentages," "SOC 2 or an indemnity cap is not invented for anyone," "we do not compare vendors with Example Co." An instruction says what NOT to do — do it silently. If you want to explain a methodology principle to the reader, write a statement about what IS in the article: "indemnity terms are given where the vendor publishes them; where it does not, the article says so."

**3. Confidence labels and provenance** ([live-fetch], [search-extract], [undocumented], checkedAt, "press release only") are working notes for the author. They cannot appear in prose in any form, except when the platform is technical and the brief explicitly asks to show the level of confirmation.

## Russian articles: language

The rules above, and the evidence and the cards, are written in English, so on a Russian article the model produces a mixture. A first run typically needs QA to clean out Latin-script words and calques by the dozen, for example: finance ops, sign-off, scope, confirmation, approved, on hold, next step, fee, consolidation, GL, misfit, flat, starts at, bulk approval, per-contractor, онбординг, апрув. Cards may still contain whole English phrases ("Northwind directly engages and contracts the contractor, uncapped indemnity").

- **Keep in Latin script only:** vendor and product names (Agent of Record, Employer of Record, EOR, Contractor Management, Accounts Payable, Example Workspace), names of tax forms (W-9, W-8BEN), abbreviations of standards (SOC 2, ISO 27001), and English interface labels quoted once as labels. Everything else goes in Russian.
- **No calques or translated syntax:** «это позволяет вам», «в конце дня», «сделать разницу», piles of genitive cases. A Russian term is preferable to an anglicism when one exists: not «онбординг» but «подключение исполнителя»; not «апрув» but «согласование»; not «фи» but «комиссия».
- **Do not carry over a single English fragment from the evidence or the cards verbatim.** A quote from a source may stay in the original language only as an explicit quote; in all other cases the fact is formulated in Russian.
- **Instruction echo is forbidden in Russian too.** The same ban as in the English block above, but the model reproduces it in other forms: «отсутствие публикации не дорисовываем и не прячем», «где вендор не раскрыл факт, стоит уточнять письменно», «как плюс, а не как единственный балл». The rule is followed silently.
- **The data date in the text and the verification dates in the cards must match.** An article must not claim «данные на <дата>» while the cards carry a later verification date.
- **Lettered numbering of subheadings (a./b./c.) is a template artefact**; it does not occur in Russian text.

## Length: what is actually hard

For the blog the writer gets no numeric target at all: not an overall size, not a range of competitors, not a minimum, not a section budget. It writes each section until every item of the substantive contract is fully covered and stops without padding or repetition. If an old job still carries a numeric budget, the writer ignores it. `scripts/wordcount.py` runs after writing for analytics and normalised style metrics, not to steer length. Hard ceilings of other platforms, for example a platform with a character limit, continue to apply in their own tracks; they do not apply to the blog.

## Quotation marks and contrasts: limits

A typical machine-written Russian article, before these rules, carries **an «а не» construction in roughly every sixth sentence** and **a pair of quotation marks in roughly every seventh** (illustrative proportions). Rules written only in English form (`X, not Y`, `scare quotes`) do not fire in Russian. The thresholds below are hard.

**The contrast "X, а не Y"** (and the variants "не X, а Y", "вопрос не в X, а в Y"): **about one per thousand words** (the earlier hard threshold of "three per article" was derived from a Russian article where the construction stood in every sixth sentence, and on a long English text it cut off normal rhetoric). And only where the reader actually confuses X and Y. Separating categories (payroll versus contractor agreements) is a legitimate case. "Нужен один архив договоров, а не просто папка" is not legitimate: it is decoration, and is written directly: «бухгалтерии нужен один архив договоров». Test: if you remove the second part and the meaning does not suffer, the construction is superfluous.

**Quotation marks**: **no more than five pairs per article**, and only for:
- a verbatim quote from a vendor page or a document;
- the first introduction of a term that is then used without quotation marks;
- the name of a status or button in an interface, if it matters for the description («подписано», «на согласовании») — and only once, without quotation marks afterwards.
Quotation marks are forbidden: as irony or distancing from an ordinary word («договоре», «бумажки», «от», «просто папка», «зелёный»); around a turn of phrase the author invented; around someone else's line that nobody said («пришлите договор и реквизиты» — if it is not a quote from a letter, remove the quotation marks and write that they request it).

Typography depends on the language. In Russian text the outer quotation marks are `«…»`, nested ones `„…“`. In English the outer quotation marks are `“…”`, nested ones `‘…’`. Straight double quotes `"..."` do not go into publication text. In English articles quotation marks are likewise used only for a quote or the first introduction of a term; `X, not Y` constructions: about one per thousand words.
